AI native developer

19 April 2026 13 min read ai · development · agents

A Different Role, Not a Faster One

A staff engineer’s day used to be easy to describe.

In the morning, you would work through a hard implementation detail. After lunch, you would review design choices with the team. In the afternoon, you would merge code, answer questions, and move on to the next change.

The visible work and the valuable work were usually strongly correlated.

Today the calendar looks similar, but the substance has changed.

In the morning, you tighten an agent instruction file, add a lint rule, or refine an invariant that the model keeps violating. After lunch, you review several generated changes to find where the system is drifting away from intent. The most valuable thing you merge may be a harness, an eval, or a permission boundary.

The temptation in boardrooms is to read this as the same role at higher throughput: same engineer, more PRs, finally the productivity numbers roll over. That reading is wrong.

The work itself has changed.

The AI-native developer is a different role from a traditional developer, not just the faster version of one. Value no longer comes from typing code but from specifying intent, designing feedback loops, bounding agent autonomy, and holding responsibility no model can hold.

Nobody knows where this lands.

What follows is how I think the role is changing, viewed from the engineering side rather than the vendor side. None of it is settled. Some claims might be wrong.

The Split

For some time, technical depth and execution speed were correlated. The engineers who understood the system best were often the ones who shipped fastest, because they saw the path more clearly. They knew where the sharp edges were, made fewer wrong turns, and moved quickly without getting lost.

That correlation made today’s evaluation systems tolerable.

PR count was noisy, but it often tracked real contribution. Code volume was already a bad metric, but not always a fatal one, because depth and speed tended to move together.

That is no longer true.

Coding agents broke that correlation in eighteen months. Speed is now available without depth, because typing is rented. The artefact (code that compiles, passes its own tests, merges before lunch) has stopped being a reliable signal of the engineering underneath. Marco Somma’s framing of two skill axes that no longer move together is the polite version [1]. The blunt version is that seniors are watching less experienced engineers outshipping them with management’s blessing, knowing the bill will arrive later.

Agents can now produce speed without corresponding depth, and dashboards cannot tell the difference.

Gergely Orosz, watching the same shift across the engineering organisations he tracks, calls it the move “from how to what”, and notes, less cheerfully, that less-adept engineers are upleveling on the dashboard while generating prodigious volumes of “AI slop” [2].

The 2025 DORA report on AI-assisted development, drawing on five thousand practitioners, names the mechanism without ornament: AI is an amplifier. Without strong controls, it warns, more change leads to more instability [3]. Translated: organisations using output volume as a levelling signal are industrialising the production of incidents.

So PR throughput, story-point velocity, and lines shipped have gone from imperfect signals to misleading ones. They flatter shallow work and discount the exact judgement that has become scarce. They reward the developer whose agent is loudest, not the one whose system survives.

The damage shows in hiring and promotion. A funnel built around visible output now risks inverted selection. It can filter for people who are good at producing activity and miss the people who are good at constraining systems, designing reliable feedback loops, and catching plausible nonsense before it enters production.

The Hands-On Reversal for Senior ICs

A counter-intuitive shift concerns senior individual contributors and tech leads.

For the last decade, common wisdom about staff and principal engineers was that they scale through influence, architecture, and mentorship. They should not disappear entirely from implementation, but the centre of gravity moved upward and outward. Stay out of the weeds. Think bigger. Multiply others.

This default no longer holds. 2026 is the year staff-plus engineers need to get hands-on again [4].

Nostalgia for craftsmanship has nothing to do with it; the reason is where the leverage now sits.

The agent’s ceiling is the spec, the harness, and the feedback loop around it. Designing those well requires the deepest mental model in the room.

In practice, a senior IC has to move closer to the work. They need not hand-write everything, but the parts that most determine quality now sit around the generation loop, and those parts are too important to delegate blindly. That work belongs with the people who hold the deepest mental model of the system, because that model is what the harness has to encode.

The inverted pitch (seniors as airborne reviewers, agents as the new junior cohort) has been tried enough in the last two years to draw a curve. The seniors who delegated execution and stayed out of the loop went blind in a short time. They could no longer tell when an agent had produced a plausibly shaped solution that missed the actual constraint. They lost the felt sense of where the system pushes back. The mental model that made them senior was built on friction with the code, but the friction is gone.

Mario Zechner names it well: be in the code, because the act of writing it, or watching it being built up step by step, is what tells you what you are building [5]. His bridge-engineer analogy is clear: a bridge engineer does not lay the bricks. They are also not in the office reviewing photos of the bridge. They are on site, watching, asking the wrong questions on purpose, refusing to sign the certificate until the inspection regime has actually caught something.

Seniors who stay remote will spend 2026 catching up.

The New Skill Stack

The answer to “what does the new role look like on a Tuesday morning” is less glamorous than marketing suggests. Five things make up most of it.

Constraint architecture.

The most valuable commit a senior makes today is likely a guardrail in an agent configuration file, a lint rule, or a CI check. Ernie Miller’s line, “the model didn’t get better, the loop got tighter”, captures the move [6]. Telling the agent what to do in prose is the weakest form of instruction. Encoding the rule in something the agent cannot ignore is the strong form. Linters do not sleep. CI does not have a bad week.

Harness engineering.

Birgitta Böckeler at Thoughtworks has proposed the cleanest taxonomy that has so far stuck: the harness is guides (feedforward: context, prompts, examples, docs) and sensors (feedback: tests, evals, telemetry, lint) [7]. An engineer’s product surface now includes the harness as well as the application: everything that narrows the space of acceptable output before the model has the chance to be too “creative”.

The goal is convergence, not elegance.

Evaluation as a first-class deliverable.

Golden sets, regression suites, behavioural evals: first-class artefacts owned by the engineer. Kent Beck has been clear about why TDD becomes a superpower in agentic settings: tests are the spec the agent cannot subvert. He has also documented agents deleting tests to make them pass, which is the kind of detail an AI-native developer must never forget [8].

An agent will optimise against whatever can be checked. If the checks are weak, the system will drift. If the checks are strong, the loop converges.

Bounding agent autonomy.

Isolation, scoped permissions, reversible operations, and auditability are no longer side concerns. Often these are still discussed too casually, as if more capable models will dissolve the problem. They will not. The question is under what conditions an agent may act, with which blast radius, and how a human regains control when the action is wrong.

The discipline is closer to running a small team of fast, eager, occasionally dishonest junior contractors than to using a tool. Nobody who has run such a team for more than a month trusts the AI hype any more.

Holding responsibility no model can hold.

ASCE Policy Statement 573 puts it plainly: “AI cannot be held accountable, nor can it replace the training, experience, and judgement of a professional engineer.” [9] The UK Financial Reporting Council was even shorter: “You can’t blame it on the box.” [10] That line should sit under every enterprise AI discussion.

This is probably the least visible skill and the least replaceable one.

The composite picture is unromantic. A senior IC is becoming part platform engineer, part tech lead, part hostage negotiator.

The Talent Pipeline

If our role demands judgement and tacit knowledge, and if execution (the substrate juniors traditionally built those on) has been outsourced to a model, the pipeline question stops being abstract.

Drawing on ADP payroll microdata covering millions of workers, the authors of Canaries in the Coal Mine [11] document a divergence that is hard to misinterpret: software developers aged 22–25 saw employment fall roughly 20% from a late-2022 peak through July 2025. Workers 30 and over in the same high-AI-exposure category do not show this pattern. The authors offer the careful interpretation that tacit knowledge defends older workers. A more direct interpretation, also supported by the data, is that companies have decided juniors are optional and are betting they can hire from a pool that has not yet noticed it is shrinking.

Stack Overflow’s 2025 analysis reports tech internships down 30% from 2023, with 57% of hiring managers stating they trust AI work over the work of interns and recent graduates. The platform’s own line reads like a sigh: if you don’t hire junior developers, you’ll someday never have senior developers [12].

This is not an argument anyone should have had to write down in 2026. The fact that it had to be written down loudly suggests something about the quality of the strategic thinking happening in some of the rooms.

The implication is that a pipeline optimised for short-term output is starving the next generation of the exact thing that makes senior engineers valuable.

How does taste get built when execution is delegated? Nobody knows yet.

Rotation through harness, eval, and guardrail work? Explicit reading of senior engineers’ mental models as artefacts? Protected agent-off exercises?

Uncharted territory. There is no experience yet on how the junior-to-senior pipeline changes under heavy AI mediation. University curricula have not adapted in any obvious way. This is a known unknown, and the industry is not funding it in proportion to its importance.

Missing Voices

The discourse on the AI-native developer role has a missing-voice problem, and it cuts in one direction. Engineers maintaining payments solutions, clinical systems, avionics, telco cores, energy, and defence software are mostly silent in public.

That absence is not random. It reflects constraint, policy, and accountability structures that make casual claims hard to publish and harder to put into practice.

Where those voices do appear, they sound different.

In aerospace, Andrew Longhurst’s discussion of DO-178C is clarifying because it is so concrete: traceability, independent review, deterministic verification, approved standards [13]. That is what accountability looks like in a regulated shop, where incidents can make the news. AI is not irrelevant there, but some popular agent workflow rhetoric sounds unserious next to it.

The same asymmetry appears in regulation. The FDA’s 2025 draft guidance on AI-enabled device software is already framing the problem in lifecycle terms [14]. NIST’s new critical infrastructure profile work is doing the same [15].

The case law is still thin to nonexistent. The regulatory direction is not.

Another reason for the silence may simply be that the discourse, as it exists, is an unproductive room for a certain type of professional engineer. It is dominated by people selling agent products and people using agents on weekend projects, and conversations with both tend to be biased.

So voices that ought to be very informative are not entering the conversation. This absence of regulated-industry first-person writing is itself the data.

The role description for the AI-native developer currently being normalised rests on assumptions that might not hold for systems with external accountability. Importing it wholesale into critical environments is a category error, not a tactical mistake.

Identity, Grief, and the Professional Posture

There is also a personal layer.

Some developers grieve the loss of craft, others the loss of context, others certainty itself. Sean Goedecke’s honesty matters here: “It sucks. I miss feeling like my job was secure.” [18]

Orchard’s point is that different people feel different losses because they valued different parts of the work [16]. Thomas Vilhena’s version is more detached and perhaps more realistic: most software work was never cathedral-building. Much of it was digital plumbing [17].

That observation should not be read as contempt. It is part of what makes the moment emotionally complicated. Some of the drudgery really is drudgery. And still, something meaningful shifts when the centre of professional value moves away from direct construction and the creativity it held.

The engineers most disoriented by the shift are usually the ones whose self-worth rests on writing code well. They need to recalibrate.

The mature posture accepts that writing code has become a commodity.

Taste, judgement, and the ability to know when to stop (the things that used to make senior engineers valuable on top of their speed at writing code) are now the only things that make them valuable.

Where does this leave us?

Our role is changing, and the unknowns inside the change are real.

Our work will produce fewer lines of implementation and more lines of instruction. Our value will come from shaping what surrounds generation (the spec, the harness, the eval, the rollback) and from carrying the responsibility that no model can carry for us.

The judgement this takes is learned the same way it always has been: by writing enough wrong code, operating enough broken systems, and being close enough to the failure modes to develop taste about them. None of those inputs got cheaper because generation did.

Our organisations need to stop measuring output volume.

Juniors who are not being hired today are the seniors who will not exist in 2031.

We senior ICs and tech leads need to be deep in the code, not above it.

The engineers who run critical systems need to enter the discourse, and the discourse should be a place where their answers are welcome. Their silence must not be mistaken for agreement.

The AI-native developer is not the engineer who ships the most with a model. It is the engineer whose system still tells the truth about itself after the model has been working on it for six months.
